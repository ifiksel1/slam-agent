Incident review Dataflash log 49 25 Aug 2026 ArduCopter 4.6.3 · mRoPixracerPro · QUAD/X

Hard landing and uncommanded re‑liftoff under LAND mode

A GPS-denied quadcopter running FAST-LIO external navigation entered LAND on an EKF failsafe, struck the ground at 3.7 g, and then climbed back to 0.84 m before the pilot disarmed. Both events trace to a single measurable cause.

FAST-LIO's odometry output latency grew from 82 ms to 669 ms during the flight. ArduPilot was told the vision measurement was 75 ms old (VISO_DELAY_MS) and structurally cannot compensate for more than 250 ms. The EKF therefore fused every vision fix at the wrong point in its time history.

The vehicle descended at 1.1 m/s while its own estimate showed 0.28 m/s, so the controller never arrested the sink. After impact, the late height estimate collapsed below the still-descending LAND target, and the position controller did the only thing it can with a below-target altitude error: it added throttle.

The lidar, the optical flow sensor, the rangefinders and the Lua scripts were all healthy and are ruled out. No component reported a fault at any point.

A sweep of all 22 recorded flights found this was the only one to diverge. The stack normally runs at ~70 ms with margin to spare and lost it to a load threshold rather than a regression — which is why nothing looked wrong beforehand.

What happened

The vehicle was hovering indoors in LOITER at roughly 1 m. All navigation — position, velocity, height and yaw — came from FAST-LIO over MAVROS; there was no GPS, and neither the barometer nor the two downward rangefinders were fused into the state estimate.

Root cause: the estimate ran late

Measured as the interval between a scan's own timestamp and the moment its pose was published, FAST-LIO's output latency grows linearly through the flight and then saturates. The two controls in the same recording rule out any measurement artefact: /lidar_points holds a flat 52 ms, and /mavros/local_position/pose — written by the same recorder on the same machine — sits at 1.2 ms.

The cause is a small, sustained real-time overrun rather than a crash or a stall. Scan load is roughly 115,000 points at 20 Hz. As the vehicle climbed and the map densified, matched points rose from ~4,900 to ~7,200 and solve time from 25 ms to 37 ms — leaving no margin inside the 50 ms budget. The pipeline ended up running about 8% over, so the backlog accumulated at ~74 ms per second until the input queue saturated near 0.6 s.

Why no amount of tuning fixes this on the flight controller

VISO_DELAY_MS has a documented range of 0–250 ms, and AP_NavEKF3_core.cpp hard-caps what it will honour:

maxTimeDelay_ms = MAX(maxTimeDelay_ms, MIN(visual_odom->get_delay_ms(), 250));

Against an actual 332 ms mean and 669 ms peak, the EKF was fusing each measurement at the wrong point in its time history no matter how the parameter was set. This has to be fixed on the companion computer.

This was not the normal state — 22 flights compared

Every FAST-LIO recording on the drive was measured the same way. The stack is normally healthy: 21 of 22 flights hold 67–78 ms from start to finish, with peaks under 90 ms, across runs up to two minutes. The crash flight is the only in-flight divergence in six weeks.

Points per scan is identical at 115,456 in every flight — the input load never varied. What varies is the match count, and it separates the flights cleanly: everything up to 5,213 matched stayed flat, including several August flights; the crash flight ran 4,709–7,271 and diverged. The pipeline has headroom to roughly 5,200 matched points and loses it before 7,300. That is a load threshold, not decay over calendar time.

One recording quantifies the headroom exactly. 08_25_20_44_47 opens with a 13.6-second backlog draining linearly to 78 ms over 9.1 s; working back from the slope, FAST-LIO was processing at ~2.5× realtime at that map load. The crash flight had it at ~0.93×. Effective throughput swings by roughly 2.7× with match count — which is why 21 flights look perfect and one falls apart.

Three flights on 13 July already showed transient spikes to 206–313 ms against a flat 72 ms baseline. The margin was being touched six weeks before it was lost.

A second, separate hazard: cold start

That 13.6-second backlog is a startup transient. For the first ~9 seconds after FAST-LIO begins publishing, its output was up to 13.6 seconds stale — arriving punctually at 20 Hz, so nothing on the flight controller could detect it. Arming inside that window would put the vehicle in the air navigating on 13-second-old position. It is a different failure mode from the one that caused the crash, and unlike that one it is entirely preventable before takeoff.

Confirmed against the companion's own recording

The flight controller log and the FAST-LIO bag are independent recordings on independent clocks, so the ordering above is only an argument until they are put on one timeline. Aligning them by cross-correlating SLAM north against the VISP records in the flight controller log gives an RMS mismatch of 3 mm across 633 samples — the aircraft received precisely what FAST-LIO published. Nothing is corrupted in transport. Only late.

The companion moves first, by about three seconds. Latency is flat at 82–84 ms from T−5 through T+3, lifts at T+5, and is already at 210 ms by T+8. The aircraft's XKF4.SP does not budge until T+9. The cause is visible on the companion three seconds before the consequence is visible on the vehicle.

The trigger is load, and it is in the table. Matched points climb 4,918 → 6,626 across T−5 to T+7, and latency departs at exactly the point that count crosses roughly 6,000. The climb after takeoff brought more of the room into view, the match count rose about 30%, and the pipeline went over its 50 ms budget.

And eigmin never degrades — it improves, 1.02M → 1.68M, as the map densifies. Both of the detector's original dimensions were moving in the direction it reads as healthier while the aircraft was losing its position estimate. That is the blind spot, measured on the flight where it mattered.

Two smaller things the joint view exposes. From T+13 the latency plateaus at 530–640 ms while SP keeps compounding to 19 — the companion's problem stopped worsening, the aircraft's did not, because error accumulates against a stale reference. And after the impact latency is still 585–635 ms: it never drained, which is the evidence behind escalating to a restart rather than waiting indefinitely on the optical-flow failover.

One caveat on the match count: it is noisy second to second (5,044–7,044 across this run), so “crossed about 6,000” is a correlation within one flight, not a calibrated trigger — the fleet sweep put the safe ceiling nearer 5,200. Latency remains the thing to trigger on. Match count is the explanation, not the detector.

The failsafe needed two witnesses and got one twice

ekf_check declares a variance failsafe only when two of {mag, velocity, position} exceed FS_EKF_THRESH together for a full second. The rule exists to demand corroboration — one sensor going bad should not ground an aircraft. In log 49 the pair was SP and SM, and both are FAST-LIO.

XKF3.IMX/IMY/IMZ are identically zero for the whole flight — the compass is never fused, because EK3_SRC1_YAW=6 takes yaw from external nav. Yet SM still climbs to 1.44. The reason is one line in AP_NavEKF3_Outputs.cpp:499:

magVar.x = sqrtF(MAX(magTestRatio.x, yawTestRatio));

With magTestRatio pinned at zero, SM is yawTestRatio: the ExternalNav yaw innovation. The field that reads as a magnetometer check is a second look at the same failing sensor, and the EKF yaw is visibly dragging behind the vision yaw through the same window — 23.4° against 27.8° at T+11.

So the two-of-three rule was satisfied by one witness voting twice. That is not a defect in ekf_check; it is what happens when a single sensor is assigned POSXY, POSZ, VELXY, VELZ and YAW at once. The corroboration the check is built around does not exist on this vehicle, and the failsafe therefore fires on evidence weaker than its design assumes — which here meant LAND.

Velocity was the one channel that stayed clean, which is the opposite of what the latency mechanism first suggests. Position error under lag grows as v·Δt; velocity error grows as a·Δt. The manoeuvre was a sustained 3.3 m/s translation at near-constant velocity, so the position term dominated while the velocity term stayed small — a lagged velocity of a constant-velocity motion is still correct; a lagged position never is. The arithmetic closes: at T+12.9 the EKF sat 0.66 m ahead of the vision position, against 287 ms of uncompensated lag × 3.3 m/s ≈ 0.95 m. SP is that offset and little else.

03

How a late estimate became a bounce

The flight controller had two sources of height information and used only one of them. The rangefinder was accurate throughout and invisible to the state estimator.

The descent was four times faster than the estimate showed
Over T+21.3 → T+22.9 the rangefinder fell 2.45 → 0.11 m — 1.1 m/s. The EKF showed 1.79 → 1.18 m, just 0.28 m/s. The controller believed it was descending slower than its 0.5 m/s target, so it kept trimming throttle down instead of arresting the sink.
Impact went unnoticed by the estimator
At 3.7 g of vertical deceleration the EKF altitude still read 1.18 m against a true 0.11 m. Nothing in the state estimate registered that the vehicle had arrived.
The backlog then drained while the vehicle sat still
Over the next second the delayed height finally caught up and collapsed 1.04 → 0.00 m — a metre of apparent descent by an airframe that was motionless on the floor.
LAND kept ramping its target down through the floor
land_run_vertical_control() had been lowering the target at LAND_SPEED the whole time. By T+24.1 s the target sat 0.65 m above the reported altitude, and continued down through zero to −0.15 m.
The controller did the only correct thing with that error
A position controller holding an altitude 0.65 m below target adds throttle. It lifted to 0.84 m AGL and was still climbing when the pilot disarmed.

Three safeguards that should have stopped it, and why none did

  • The land detector never latched. It requires motors->limit.throttle_lower, and the controller was commanding climb — so throttle sat at 0.29–0.47 against a hover value of 0.388. The false climb demand suppresses the very detector that would have disarmed the vehicle. Its rangefinder gate was satisfied at 0.06 m; that was not the blocker.
  • Nothing independent could contradict the estimate. EK3_SRC1_POSZ=6 (ExternalNav) and EK3_RNG_USE_HGT=-1 mean the barometer and both rangefinders are invisible to the EKF. The flight controller was reading 0.06 m on a healthy sensor and structurally could not act on it.
  • The pilot could not cancel LAND from the sticks. PILOT_THR_BHV=5 leaves the high-throttle-cancels-land bit clear, and FLTMODE_CH=0 disables RC mode switching. Disarming was the only lever available.

A note on the automatic baro fallback

ArduPilot does have one: selectHeightForFusion() reverts to the barometer when external-nav height is lost. It keys on arrival time — extNavDataIsFresh = age < 500 ms — and external nav kept arriving at 20 Hz with a largest gap of 0.31 s. The data was punctual and wrong, which is the one case this check cannot catch. The same blind spot defeats VISO_DELAY_MS compensation and the companion's own drift monitor.

04

Ruled out

Each of these was a plausible alternative explanation and each is excluded by direct evidence.

Most importantly, the vehicle does have an automatic SLAM-failure response: drift_monitor.py on the companion, which on a CRITICAL verdict switches the EKF to optical flow via MAV_CMD_DO_AUX_FUNCTION (func 90) and restarts FAST-LIO. It has fired automatically in the field (log 46) and on the pilot's CH9 switch (log 47, twice), completing a failover-and-recover cycle in 5.3–5.6 s. It was running throughout the crash flight and correctly reported OK — because its detector measures geometric observability, and the geometry never degraded.

Lidar sensor 20 Hz, ~115,000 points per scan, zero dropped frames, zero gaps. Publish latency a flat 52 ms for the entire recording.
Optical flow Healthy the whole time — ~22 Hz, quality 34/57.7/78 (min/mean/max). Not used on this flight — source set 1 was active throughout — but proven in service: drift_monitor failed over to it cleanly in logs 46 and 47.
Source-switch script The SPLIT_ARCADE_MODE Lua fired once, at log t=2166.2 — 37 s after disarm. It guards on not arming:is_armed(), so it cannot select the null source set in flight. v1.8 was flown; v1.10 now on the card keeps that guard and adds a real prearm block.
VOXL toggle RC9 sat flat at 1050 µs for all 486 samples and never crossed its 1500 µs threshold. Whatever it drives on the companion was never triggered.
SLAM self-assessment drift_monitor.py reported OK throughout. Its floors are eig_min<5,000 and matched<150; actuals were 989k–1.53M and 4,709–7,271 — 200× and 31× clear. Every test is a lower bound, and none involves time.
ExternalNav velocity fusion Sending FAST-LIO's velocity to the flight controller as VISION_SPEED_ESTIMATE was suspected of over-weighting a measurement correlated with the position it is fused alongside. It played no part here: XKF4.SV read 0.34 at the moment the failsafe declared and did not hold above 0.80 until T+18.3 — after LAND had already been commanded.
LAND mode logic Executed correctly on every count: 4 s pause, 0.5 m/s target ramp, position hold, land-detector gating. It was fed a bad estimate, not miscoded.

Configuration findings

Found while reading the live parameter set, re-confirmed against the flight controller after the sweep. The first is independent of this incident and is the most serious.

FS_EKF_ACTION0 Not a legal value on 4.6.3 — the documented set is 1/2/3. It falls into default: in failsafe_ekf_event() and triggers LAND. "0 = Report only" exists only on 4.7 and master. This is what put the vehicle into LAND.
EK3_SRC1_POSZ6 Height comes entirely from SLAM. With EK3_RNG_USE_HGT=-1 there is no independent vertical reference at any altitude.

Recommendations

  1. Add a latency term to drift_monitor's detector
    The failover mechanism already exists and is proven; it is the detector that is blind. _evaluate() tests only lower bounds on eig_min and matched_pts, and nothing in the node's 366 lines compares a header stamp to the clock. Subscribe to /Odometry, compute now − header.stamp, and WARN at ~150 ms / CRIT at ~250 ms — ArduPilot's compensation ceiling — reusing the existing K=5 hysteresis. A CRITICAL verdict then routes into _execute_restart(), which already switches to optical flow and restarts FAST-LIO in about five seconds.
    Companion · drift_monitor.py
  2. Publish latency on the risk topic and gate arming on it
    Latency exceeded 200 ms a full 10 s before the failsafe and 15 s before impact — ample warning, entirely unmeasured. The arming gate matters separately: the interlock currently relies on vision dropping during a restart, which does not cover vision that is present but 13.6 s stale, as it was when the vehicle armed and took off in log 50.
    Companion · drift_monitor.py
  3. Stamp vision messages with the measurement time, not the publish time
    The bridge re-stamps with rospy.Time.now() at publish, so everything upstream of it is erased from the flight controller's view. The FC's own measured age — VISP/VISV CTimeMS against system time — peaked at 376 ms while the companion was in fact 669 ms late. The aircraft was handed roughly half the real lag and told the rest was VISO_DELAY_MS=75. The fix is not to revert the re-stamp, which solved a genuine clock-domain bug, but to carry lidar_end_time translated into the synced clock so ArduPilot can compensate honestly up to its 250 ms ceiling. Velocity is the worse case: it arrives a median 53 ms staler than position (against 10 ms) despite sharing one header stamp, and both are compensated with the same constant.
    Companion · fastlio_mavros_bridge.py
  4. Set FS_EKF_ACTION deliberately to 1 or 2
    One parameter, immediate effect, independent of everything else here. Whatever the intent behind 0 was, on this firmware it is the most aggressive available response.
    Flight controller
  5. Fix the barometer before relying on it
    It swings 5.99 m with the props turning (σ 1.24 m) against 0.39 m at rest — prop wash and ground effect, not sensor failure. That makes it useless as the fallback every height-source scheme depends on, including the one below.
    Hardware
  6. Give the vertical loop an independent source — once the baro is fixed
    Target EK3_SRC1_POSZ=2 with EK3_RNG_USE_HGT=70: inertial height in cruise, rangefinder only in the last few metres, with the built-in speed and terrain-stability gates active. Prefer this to POSZ=1, which makes the EKF's altitude terrain-relative — the vehicle then balloons over any object beneath it, and the speed gate does not apply. For obstacle clearance use SURFTRAK_MODE=1, which moves the controller's target and leaves the reference inertial.
    Flight controller
  7. Bound matched points from above, not just below
    Every threshold in the detector guards against too few features. This failure was caused by too many: the match count rose 4,709 → 7,271 and took the pipeline over its real-time budget, while the monitor read that same rising number as maximally healthy. Cap map density, and answer why the 25 August scene produced 40% more matches than any previous flight.
    Companion
  8. Make the CH9 recovery discoverable
    The pilot had a proven one-switch recovery — CH9 triggers the same failover, demonstrated twice 18 hours earlier — and RC9 sat at 1050 µs throughout the crash. Nothing told them to reach for it. Whatever telemetry the latency term produces has to reach the pilot in the air, not just the log.
    Operations
  9. Separate the routine EKF warning from the real one
    Eight of the nine EKF variance events across these logs were self-inflicted by the tank-mode switch selecting the null source set, all while disarmed. The ninth was real, in flight, and crashed the aircraft — and looked identical. Suppress or re-word the expected one so the exceptional one is legible.
    Operations
  10. Restore the VisualOdometry arming check; give the pilot an abort
    Bit 16 of ARMING_CHECK, plus PILOT_THR_BHV bit 1 and a mode switch or aux LAND. Real but last-resort: it depends on a human reacting within a few seconds.
    Flight controller

Method and evidence

  • Parameters read live from the flight controller over MAVLink — 1138 values, full set.
  • Dataflash logs 49 and 50 downloaded over MAVLink at ~550 kB/s, both verified complete with zero missing bytes.
  • Log 49 re-pulled over MAVLink at 790 kB/s (15,499,069 bytes, byte-complete) to read XKF3, XKF4, VISP and VISV directly — the innovation test ratios that decide the failsafe, and the flight controller's own measurement of vision age, neither of which the companion bag can show.
  • Lua scripts recovered from the logs themselves via FILE messages rather than the SD card. lua_scripts.cpp:309 logs every script that loads successfully unless SCR_DEBUG_OPTS suppresses it, and it is 0 here — so the three recovered scripts are the complete set. The logs also carry @SYS/storage.bin, a byte-exact 32 KB snapshot of the parameter EEPROM as it stood during the flight.
  • FAST-LIO bag 08_25_2026_20_38_04_flight, correlated to the flight controller clock through the log's own "recording started / finished" messages; the 32.2 s bag span matches the 33.4 s logged interval. Read with ROS Noetic in a container.
  • Fleet sweep across all 24 flight bags on the drive (22 carried odometry), measuring publish latency as bag-record time minus header stamp — the same method, and the same two in-bag controls, as the crash flight.
  • Lua scripts also pulled live from the SD card over MAVLink FTP, which is how the v1.8-flown / v1.10-current discrepancy surfaced.
  • Behavioural claims checked against the ArduCopter 4.6.3 source at the tagged release, not against current master — several relevant parameters were renamed or re-valued afterwards.
